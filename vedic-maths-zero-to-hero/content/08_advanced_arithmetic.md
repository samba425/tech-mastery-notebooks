# Part VIII — Advanced Arithmetic

This part covers the arithmetic topics that make up most of the quantitative-aptitude syllabus in SSC, Banking and Railway exams. For every topic the **standard concept** comes first, then the **mental-math shortcuts** that genuinely help.

:::note An honest note on shortcuts
Most aptitude topics do **not** have a special Vedic sutra. What helps is: clear formulas, the fraction–percentage chart, LCM thinking for work problems, ratio thinking, and the fast multiplication/division methods from Parts III–VI. We do not attach Vedic names where they do not belong.
:::

## Chapter 25: Ratio, Proportion and Averages

### 25.1 Ratio

A **ratio** a : b compares two quantities of the same kind. It can be simplified like a fraction: 18 : 24 = 3 : 4.

[[diagram:bar_model 5 3 ₹4,800]]

:::example Dividing in a ratio
**Divide ₹7,200 among A, B, C in the ratio 5 : 3 : 4.** Total parts = 12; one part = 7,200 ÷ 12 = 600. A = **₹3,000**, B = **₹1,800**, C = **₹2,400**.
:::

:::formula Combining ratios
If A : B = 2 : 3 and B : C = 4 : 5, make B the same: A : B = 8 : 12, B : C = 12 : 15 → **A : B : C = 8 : 12 : 15**.
:::

### 25.2 Direct and inverse proportion

:::formula Two kinds of proportion
**Direct:** both increase together; the ratio stays constant. 6 pens cost ₹90 → 15 pens cost 90 × 15/6 = **₹225**.
**Inverse:** one increases as the other decreases; the product stays constant. 12 workers take 15 days → 18 workers take 12 × 15/18 = **10 days**.
:::

:::tip The "men-days-work" formula
M₁ × D₁ × H₁ / W₁ = M₂ × D₂ × H₂ / W₂ (men, days, hours per day, work). One formula handles most "chain rule" questions.
:::

### 25.3 Averages

Average = sum of values ÷ number of values.

:::tip Assumed-mean (deviation) method
Choose a convenient value near the data, average the **deviations**, and add. Average of 47, 52, 49, 55, 57: assume 50; deviations −3, 2, −1, 5, 7 → total 10 → 10 ÷ 5 = 2. Average = **52**.
:::

:::example A number removed
The average of 5 numbers is 42. When one number is removed, the average of the rest is 40. Removed number = 5 × 42 − 4 × 40 = 210 − 160 = **50**.
:::

### 25.4 Weighted averages

:::formula
Weighted average = (n₁a₁ + n₂a₂ + …) ÷ (n₁ + n₂ + …)
:::

:::example Two sections
30 students average 60; 20 students average 75. Combined average = (1,800 + 1,500) ÷ 50 = **66**.
**Shortcut (alligation view):** the combined average lies between 60 and 75, closer to the bigger group. Distances are in the inverse ratio of sizes 30 : 20 = 3 : 2, so the gap 15 splits as 6 : 9 → 60 + 6 = 66.
:::

## Chapter 26: Percentages in Depth

### 26.1 Percentage as a fraction (the master chart)

The fraction chart of Chapter 3 is your best tool. For example, 37.5% is 3/8, so 37.5% of 1,624 is 3 × (1,624 ÷ 8) = 3 × 203 = **609**.

### 26.2 "A is r% more than B" — how much less is B than A?

:::formula
If A is r% more than B, then B is **r/(100 + r) × 100 %** less than A.
If A is r% less than B, then B is **r/(100 − r) × 100 %** more than A.
:::

:::example Salaries
A's salary is 25% more than B's. B's salary is 25/125 × 100 = **20%** less than A's.
Price rises by 25%. To keep spending the same, consumption must fall by **20%** (same formula).
:::

### 26.3 Population and depreciation

:::formula
After n years at r% growth: P × (1 + r/100)ⁿ. At r% depreciation: P × (1 − r/100)ⁿ.
:::

:::example
A town of 50,000 grows by 10% a year. After 2 years: 50,000 × 1.1 × 1.1 = **60,500**.
A machine worth ₹80,000 depreciates 10% a year. After 2 years: 80,000 × 0.9 × 0.9 = **₹64,800**.
:::

### 26.4 Successive percentage changes

:::formula Net change of a% then b%
a + b + ab/100 (use negative numbers for decreases).
Discounts of 20% and 10%: −20 − 10 + 200/100 = −28 → a single discount of **28%**.
:::

## Chapter 27: Profit, Loss and Discount

### 27.1 Core terms

- **Cost price (CP):** what the seller paid.
- **Selling price (SP):** what the buyer pays.
- **Profit** = SP − CP (if SP > CP); **Loss** = CP − SP.
- **Profit % and loss % are always on CP.**
- **Marked price (MP):** the price label. **Discount** is always on MP.

:::formula Multiplier forms
SP = CP × (100 + profit%)/100 · SP = CP × (100 − loss%)/100 · SP = MP × (100 − discount%)/100
:::

:::example Five profit-and-loss examples
1. CP ₹800, profit 15% → SP = 800 × 1.15 = **₹920**.
2. SP ₹1,170 after a 10% loss → CP = 1,170 ÷ 0.9 = **₹1,300**.
3. MP ₹2,000, discount 15% → SP = **₹1,700**. If CP = ₹1,360, profit = 340/1,360 = **25%**.
4. Bought 12 pens for ₹100 and sold 10 pens for ₹100. CP per pen 100/12, SP per pen 100/10 → profit = (1/10 − 1/12)/(1/12) = 20%. **Shortcut:** profit % = (12 − 10)/10 × 100 = **20%**.
5. A dishonest trader sells at cost price but uses a 900 g weight for 1 kg. Profit = 100/900 × 100 = **11 1/9 %**.
:::

:::warn Profit % is on cost price
If CP = 400 and SP = 500, profit is 100, which is 25% of CP — **not** 20% (that would be on SP).
:::

:::tip Same SP, one profit and one loss
Two items sold at the same price, one at x% profit and one at x% loss, always give an **overall loss of x²/100 %**. For x = 20: loss 4%.
:::

## Chapter 28: Simple and Compound Interest

### 28.1 Simple interest

:::formula
SI = P × R × T / 100. Amount A = P + SI.
:::

:::example
₹12,000 at 8% per annum for 3 years: SI = 12,000 × 8 × 3 / 100 = **₹2,880**.
At what rate does money double in 8 years (SI)? SI = P, so R = 100/T = 100/8 = **12.5%**.
:::

### 28.2 Compound interest

:::formula
A = P(1 + R/100)ᵀ. CI = A − P.
Half-yearly: use R/2 and 2T. Quarterly: R/4 and 4T.
:::

:::example Compound interest
1. ₹10,000 at 10% for 2 years: A = 10,000 × 1.21 = 12,100 → **CI ₹2,100**.
2. ₹8,000 at 10% per annum compounded half-yearly for 1 year: A = 8,000 × 1.05² = 8,820 → **CI ₹820**.
3. ₹15,625 at 4% for 3 years: A = 15,625 × (26/25)³ = 15,625 × 17,576/15,625 = **₹17,576**. (Choosing 4% = 1/25 makes the arithmetic exact.)
:::

:::formula CI − SI shortcut
For 2 years: CI − SI = P(R/100)². For 3 years: CI − SI = P(R/100)²(3 + R/100).
₹10,000 at 10%, 2 years: 10,000 × 0.01 = **₹100** (CI 2,100 vs SI 2,000 ✓).
:::

:::tip Year-by-year layout (the "percentage tree")
10% CI on ₹10,000: year 1 interest 1,000; year 2 interest is 1,000 plus 10% of that 1,000, i.e. 1,100. Total 2,100. For 3 years add 1,000 + 200 + 10 = 1,210 → total 3,310. Each year's interest = previous interest + R% of it.
:::

## Chapter 29: Time and Work, Pipes and Cisterns

### 29.1 The LCM method

:::formula Total work = LCM of the days
Let the total work be the LCM of the individual times. Each person's daily work = total ÷ their days. Combined time = total ÷ combined daily work.
:::

:::example A in 12 days, B in 18 days
Total = LCM(12, 18) = 36 units. A does 3/day, B does 2/day; together 5/day. Time = 36/5 = **7 1/5 days**.
:::

:::example Finding one person's time
A and B together take 10 days; A alone takes 15. Total = 30 units: together 3/day, A 2/day → B 1/day → B alone **30 days**.
:::

:::example Efficiency
A is twice as efficient as B, and together they finish in 14 days. Efficiencies 2 : 1, total = 3 × 14 = 42 units. A alone: 42/2 = **21 days**; B alone: **42 days**.
:::

### 29.2 Pipes and cisterns

Filling pipes do positive work; outlets (leaks) do negative work.

:::example
Pipe A fills a tank in 20 min, B in 30 min, and C empties it in 15 min. Total = LCM(20, 30, 15) = 60 units. Per minute: +3, +2, −4 → net +1. Time = **60 minutes**.
:::

:::warn Check whether a pipe empties
If the outlet is faster than all inlets combined, the tank never fills. Read the question carefully.
:::

:::recap Chapters 25–29
- Ratios: total parts → one part → each share.
- Direct: ratio constant. Inverse: product constant.
- Averages: use deviations from an assumed mean.
- r% more ⇒ r/(100 + r) less. Successive: a + b + ab/100.
- Profit % on CP; discount % on MP.
- SI = PRT/100; A = P(1 + R/100)ᵀ; 2-year CI − SI = P(R/100)².
- Work and pipes: total work = LCM; outlets are negative.
:::
