## Chapter 3: Fractions, Decimals and Percentages

:::objective
Understand what a fraction means; convert between fractions, decimals and percentages; simplify and compare fractions; calculate percentages, percentage increase and decrease; memorise the fraction–percentage values that appear constantly in exams.
:::

### 3.1 What is a fraction?

A fraction describes **part of a whole**. In 3/4, the whole is cut into **4 equal parts** (the *denominator*) and we take **3** of them (the *numerator*).

[[diagram:fraction_bar 3 4]]

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Proper fraction | numerator < denominator | 3/8 |
| Improper fraction | numerator ≥ denominator | 11/4 |
| Mixed number | whole number + proper fraction | 2 3/4 (= 11/4) |
| Unit fraction | numerator 1 | 1/7 |

:::formula Converting mixed ↔ improper
2 3/4 = (2 × 4 + 3)/4 = 11/4.   17/5 = 3 remainder 2 = 3 2/5.
:::

### 3.2 Equivalent fractions and simplification

Multiplying or dividing the numerator **and** the denominator by the same non-zero number does not change the value: 1/2 = 2/4 = 50/100.

To **simplify**, divide both by their **HCF** (highest common factor).

:::example Simplify 84/126
HCF(84, 126) = 42. 84 ÷ 42 = 2 and 126 ÷ 42 = 3, so 84/126 = **2/3**.
If you cannot see the HCF, divide step by step: 84/126 → (÷2) 42/63 → (÷3) 14/21 → (÷7) 2/3.
:::

### 3.3 Adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing fractions

:::formula The four operations
**Add/subtract:** make the denominators equal (use the LCM), then add/subtract numerators.
**Multiply:** numerator × numerator, denominator × denominator. Cancel first to keep numbers small.
**Divide:** multiply by the reciprocal (flip the second fraction).
:::

:::example Worked examples
- 2/3 + 3/4 = 8/12 + 9/12 = 17/12 = 1 5/12
- 5/6 − 1/4 = 10/12 − 3/12 = 7/12
- 4/9 × 3/8 = (cancel 4 with 8, 3 with 9) 1/3 × 1/2 = 1/6
- 3/5 ÷ 9/10 = 3/5 × 10/9 = 30/45 = 2/3
- 1 1/2 × 2 2/3 = 3/2 × 8/3 = 8/2 = 4
:::

:::tip Vertically and crosswise for fractions
For a/b + c/d, the answer is (ad + bc)/bd — "cross multiply for the top, multiply the bottoms". 2/3 + 3/4 = (2×4 + 3×3)/(3×4) = 17/12. This is the same crosswise pattern you will meet in *Urdhva-Tiryagbhyam* (Part II). Simplify at the end.
:::

### 3.4 Decimals

A decimal is a fraction whose denominator is a power of 10: 0.7 = 7/10, 0.25 = 25/100, 0.125 = 125/1000.

- **Adding/subtracting:** line up the decimal points. 12.5 + 3.75 = 16.25
- **Multiplying:** multiply as whole numbers, then count the total decimal places. 1.2 × 0.03 → 12 × 3 = 36, three decimal places → 0.036
- **Dividing:** make the divisor a whole number by moving both points. 4.8 ÷ 0.12 = 480 ÷ 12 = 40

:::warn Decimal places
0.2 × 0.3 = 0.06, **not** 0.6. Count: one place + one place = two places.
:::

### 3.5 Fraction → decimal

Divide the numerator by the denominator. If the denominator has only the prime factors 2 and 5, the decimal **terminates** (3/8 = 0.375). Otherwise it **recurs**: 1/3 = 0.333…, 1/7 = 0.142857142857…

Part II shows two Vedic ways to find recurring decimals such as 1/19 and 1/7 quickly.

### 3.6 Percentages

**Per cent** means "per hundred". 35% means 35 out of 100 = 35/100 = 0.35.

[[diagram:percent_grid 35]]

:::formula Conversions
Fraction → % : multiply by 100. 3/8 × 100 = 37.5%
% → fraction : divide by 100 and simplify. 45% = 45/100 = 9/20
Decimal → % : move the point two places right. 0.072 = 7.2%
:::

### 3.7 Finding a percentage of a quantity

:::example Build any percentage from 10%, 5% and 1%
**Find 35% of 640.** 10% = 64, so 30% = 192; 5% = 32. Total 35% = 192 + 32 = **224**.
**Find 18% of 450.** 10% = 45; 1% = 4.5 so 8% = 36. Total = 45 + 36 = **81**.
**Find 12.5% of 488.** 12.5% = 1/8. 488 ÷ 8 = **61**.
**Find 120% of 75.** 100% = 75 and 20% = 15. Total = **90**.
**Find 0.5% of 3,000.** 1% = 30, so 0.5% = **15**.
:::

:::tip x% of y = y% of x
8% of 25 is hard? Swap: 25% of 8 = 2. This always works because x/100 × y = y/100 × x.
:::

### 3.8 Percentage increase and decrease

:::formula Percentage change
Percentage change = (change ÷ original value) × 100.
New value after an increase of r% = original × (100 + r)/100.
New value after a decrease of r% = original × (100 − r)/100.
:::

:::example Price rise
A book's price rises from ₹240 to ₹276. Change = 36. Percentage increase = 36/240 × 100 = **15%**.
:::

:::warn Up 20% then down 20% is not "no change"
₹100 → up 20% → ₹120 → down 20% → ₹96. The second percentage is taken of a *bigger* number. Net change = −4%. Formula for successive changes a% and b%: net = a + b + ab/100 (use negative values for decreases): 20 − 20 − 400/100 = −4%.
:::

:::recap Chapter 3 in five lines
- Fraction = parts taken / equal parts in the whole.
- Simplify by dividing top and bottom by the HCF.
- Add with a common denominator; multiply straight across; divide by flipping.
- Percent = per hundred; build any percentage from 10%, 5% and 1%.
- Percentage change is always measured against the **original** value.
:::

## Chapter 4: Powers, Roots and Number Properties

:::objective
Use squares, cubes and exponents; find square and cube roots of perfect powers; write numbers as products of primes; find HCF and LCM; work with remainders; estimate sensibly.
:::

### 4.1 Squares and cubes

The **square** of a number is the number times itself: 7² = 7 × 7 = 49. It is the area of a square with side 7. The **cube** is the number used three times: 4³ = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64, the volume of a cube with side 4.

[[table:squares 1 30 5]]

[[table:cubes 1 15 3]]

:::tip Squares go up by odd numbers
1, 4, 9, 16, 25, … The gaps are 3, 5, 7, 9, … So 31² = 30² + 30 + 31 = 900 + 61 = 961. In general (n + 1)² = n² + n + (n + 1).
:::

### 4.2 Exponents and powers

In 2⁵, 2 is the **base** and 5 is the **exponent** (or index). 2⁵ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32.

:::formula Laws of exponents (a ≠ 0)
aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ  ·  aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ  ·  (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ  ·  (ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ
a⁰ = 1  ·  a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ  ·  a^{1/2} = √a  ·  a^{1/3} = ∛a
:::

:::example Using the laws
- 2³ × 2⁴ = 2⁷ = 128
- 5⁶ ÷ 5⁴ = 5² = 25
- (3²)³ = 3⁶ = 729
- 10⁻³ = 1/1000 = 0.001
- 2¹⁰ = 1,024 ≈ 1,000 (useful for estimation)
:::

[[table:powers2]]

### 4.3 Square roots and cube roots

The **square root** √n is the number which, squared, gives n: √144 = 12. The **cube root** ∛n is the number which, cubed, gives n: ∛343 = 7.

[[table:roots_endings]]

:::tip Last digits identify roots
A perfect square can only end in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 or 9. A number ending in 2, 3, 7 or 8 is **never** a perfect square. Every digit 0–9 appears exactly once as the last digit of a cube — so the last digit of a perfect cube tells you the last digit of its cube root. Part VI uses this to find cube roots of 4–6 digit perfect cubes in seconds.
:::

### 4.4 Prime factorisation

Every whole number greater than 1 can be written as a product of primes in exactly one way (ignoring order). This is called the **Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic**.

[[diagram:factor_tree 360]]

360 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 5 = 2³ × 3² × 5.

:::formula Number of factors
If n = pᵃ × qᵇ × rᶜ …, the number of factors is (a + 1)(b + 1)(c + 1)…
360 = 2³ × 3² × 5¹ → (3 + 1)(2 + 1)(1 + 1) = 24 factors.
:::

### 4.5 HCF and LCM

- **HCF** (highest common factor, also GCD): the biggest number dividing all the given numbers.
- **LCM** (lowest common multiple): the smallest number that all the given numbers divide.

:::formula Using prime factors
HCF = product of common primes with the **smallest** powers.
LCM = product of all primes with the **largest** powers.
For two numbers: HCF × LCM = product of the numbers.
:::

:::example HCF and LCM of 72 and 120
72 = 2³ × 3², 120 = 2³ × 3 × 5.
HCF = 2³ × 3 = **24**. LCM = 2³ × 3² × 5 = **360**. Check: 24 × 360 = 8,640 = 72 × 120 ✓.
:::

:::tip When to use which
"Largest size of equal pieces / groups" → **HCF**. "When will they next happen together / smallest common length" → **LCM**. Bells ringing every 12 and 18 minutes ring together again after LCM(12, 18) = 36 minutes.
:::

### 4.6 Remainders and divisibility

:::formula Remainder arithmetic
The remainder of a sum (or product) equals the remainder of the sum (or product) of the remainders.
(47 × 53) ÷ 5 → remainders 2 and 3 → 2 × 3 = 6 → and 6 leaves remainder **1** on division by 5. Check: 47 × 53 = 2,491, and 2,491 ÷ 5 leaves 1 ✓.
:::

This simple idea lets you find remainders of huge numbers without calculating them, and it is the basis of digit-sum checking.

### 4.7 Approximation and estimation

:::approx Rounding rules
To round to the nearest 10, 100, …: look at the next digit to the right. 5 or more → round up; less than 5 → round down. 4,768 ≈ 4,800 (nearest hundred) ≈ 5,000 (nearest thousand).
For square roots: √50 is a bit more than 7 because 7² = 49. A good estimate is √n ≈ a + (n − a²)/(2a), where a² is the nearest perfect square: √50 ≈ 7 + 1/14 ≈ 7.07 (true value 7.0711…).
:::

:::recap Chapter 4 in six lines
- aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ; a⁰ = 1; a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ.
- Perfect squares end only in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, 9.
- Cube last digits: 2↔8 and 3↔7 swap; the others stay the same.
- Prime factorisation is unique; number of factors = product of (power + 1).
- HCF: smallest powers of common primes. LCM: largest powers of all primes.
- Remainders can be multiplied and added.
:::
